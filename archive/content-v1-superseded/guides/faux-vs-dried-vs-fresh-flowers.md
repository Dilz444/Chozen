## How do faux, dried and fresh flowers compare?

Each one wins on something different. Here's the short version.

| | Fresh | Dried | Faux |
|---|---|---|---|
| **How long it lasts** | About a week or two, depending on the flower | One to two years before it fades or gets brittle | Several years with dusting, kept out of strong sun |
| **Cost** | Lowest up front, but you buy again | Middling | Highest up front, but bought once |
| **Look** | Real colour, movement, it opens and changes | Muted, textured, rustic | Close to fresh from a distance, no change over time |
| **Scent** | Yes, for many flowers | Faint or none | None |
| **Care** | Water and trims every two days | Keep dry, dust gently | Dust every week or two |
| **Allergies** | Pollen and scent can bother some people | Little pollen, but dust and loose bits | No pollen, though dust collects |
| **Pets** | Some flowers are toxic, lilies especially | Toxic plants stay toxic when dried | Not poisonous, but small parts can be chewed |
| **End of life** | Compost, minus any wrap or foam | Compost if undyed and wire-free | Mostly plastic, rarely recyclable |

## Which looks most like the real thing?

Fresh, obviously, because it is the real thing. A well-made faux arrangement comes close, especially roses,
peonies, orchids and hydrangeas, which have firm shapes that copy well. Dried flowers aren't trying to look fresh.
They have their own look: papery, soft-coloured and textured.

## Which is best for someone with hay fever?

Faux, as there's no pollen. Dried flowers have very little, but they hold dust. If you're sending fresh, pick
low-pollen flowers such as roses, tulips, orchids or hydrangeas, and avoid lilies, chrysanthemums and daisies.

## Which is safest with pets?

Faux flowers aren't poisonous, though a determined chewer can swallow a piece of plastic or wire. With fresh and
dried, it depends on what's in them. A dried lily is just as dangerous to a cat as a fresh one. There's more in
our [flowers and pets guide](/blogs/guides/flowers-and-pets).

## Are faux flowers better for the environment?

Not simply, no. Faux flowers are made mostly from plastics and wire. They're usually made overseas, shipped a long
way and hard to recycle when you're done with them. Their case is that you buy them once and keep them for years,
where a fresh bouquet every week adds up.

Fresh flowers have their own footprint. Many are grown in heated glasshouses or flown in from Kenya, Ecuador or
Colombia, and some arrangements use floral foam, which is a plastic that breaks into microplastics. British
seasonal flowers in spring and summer have a much lighter footprint.

Dried flowers can be the lowest impact if they're grown locally and left natural. Many aren't. Bleached and dyed
dried flowers are processed with chemicals and often imported.

The fairest summary is this. Faux works best when you'll keep it for years. Fresh is lightest when it's seasonal
and foam-free. Dried is lightest when it's natural and local.

## Which should I choose as a gift?

Think about who's getting it and for how long.

- **For a moment today,** such as a birthday, a new baby or a thank you on the day, fresh flowers say it best.
  Our [same-day flowers](/collections/same-day-flowers) are hand-delivered locally.
- **For someone far away or something to keep,** faux makes sense, because it survives the post and lasts.
  See our [faux flowers](/collections/faux-flowers) and other [gifts that last](/collections/gifts-that-last).
- **For someone who loves a natural, rustic look,** dried flowers suit a shelf or mantelpiece well.

## How do I keep each kind looking good?

Fresh flowers want fresh water and a trim every two days, set out in
[how to make flowers last](/blogs/guides/how-to-make-flowers-last). Dried flowers want dry air and no sunny
windowsill. Faux flowers just need dusting, and [how to clean faux flowers](/blogs/guides/how-to-clean-faux-flowers)
covers the rest.
