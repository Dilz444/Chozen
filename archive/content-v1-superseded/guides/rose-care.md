## Why are my roses drooping at the neck?

A drooping head almost always means an air bubble has stopped water reaching the bloom. It's called bent neck,
and it can happen within a day if the roses sat out of water for a while.

To fix it, re-cut two or three centimetres off the stem, under water if you can. Then wrap the roses tightly in
paper so the heads are supported upright and stand them in deep, cool water for one to two hours. Most recover.
If a head is still hanging the next day, cut that rose short and float it in a bowl.

## How should I cut rose stems?

Use sharp secateurs or a knife and cut on a slant. Roses have woody stems that seal over quickly, so a fresh cut
every two days makes a real difference to how much they can drink. Avoid ordinary kitchen scissors if they're
blunt, because they crush the stem.

## How much water do roses need?

Fill the vase about two thirds full. Roses drink a lot on the first day, so check the level that evening. Add the
flower food sachet, change the water every two days and rinse the vase each time.

## Where should I put a vase of roses?

In the coolest room you're happy to look at them in. Warmth makes roses open fast and drop sooner. Keep them out
of direct sun, off the radiator shelf and away from ripening fruit, which gives off a gas that ages flowers.

## How long do roses last?

Five to ten days is typical. Long-stem roses with tight heads often last longer than open, frilly garden roses,
which give you scent and shape but less time. Roses that arrive in bud will open over the first two or three days.

## What can I do with roses that are past their best?

Hang them upside down in a dry, dark spot for two to three weeks and they'll dry. They darken and shrink as they
dry, but they keep. Or pull the petals for confetti or a bowl of potpourri.
