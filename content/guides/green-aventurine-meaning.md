## What does it look like?

Green aventurine ranges from pale mint to a deep bottle green. It's opaque to slightly translucent, and under a
light it shows a fine, scattered glitter. Some pieces are an even green and others are mottled or speckled. It's
usually sold tumbled, as beads, or carved into hearts, palm stones and small bowls.

## Where does it come from?

Green aventurine is quartz, usually a granular rock called quartzite, with a Mohs hardness of about 6.5 to 7. Its
colour and glitter both come from fuchsite, a green mica rich in chromium, spread through the stone in thin flat
flakes. Most green aventurine comes from India. There are smaller deposits in Brazil, Russia and Chile.

The name has an odd history. In Venice, glassmakers produced a glittering copper glass that came to be called
*avventurina*, from *a ventura*, "by chance", after an accidental discovery. The natural stone was later named
after the glass. That glass is still sold today as goldstone, and it's glass, not a mineral. Some pale stones are
dyed to look like green aventurine, which a careful seller will say on the label.

## What is it traditionally associated with?

In folklore, green aventurine is known as a lucky stone, sometimes nicknamed the gambler's stone. In crystal lore
it's associated with optimism, opportunity and fresh starts. In chakra tradition its green is matched with the
heart chakra, and modern zodiac lists often pair it with Taurus and Virgo.

## Who would you give it to?

Someone starting a new job, a new school or a new business, with a card about fresh starts. A friend taking a
driving test or moving house. A keen gardener, or anyone whose favourite colour is green. A heart or tumble stone
fits in a card envelope, so it's an easy add-on to flowers. See the
[crystals collection](/collections/crystals) for current pieces.

## How do you look after it?

Green aventurine is hard enough for everyday bracelets and rings. Wash it in lukewarm soapy water, rinse and dry
it with a soft cloth. Keep bead bracelets away from long soaks, which weaken the stretch cord rather than the
stone.

Natural green aventurine keeps its colour well in daylight. Dyed pieces can fade in strong sun, so if a label
says dyed, keep it out of bright windows. Store it apart from harder stones so the polish stays bright.
