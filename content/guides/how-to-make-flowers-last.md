## How should I cut the stems?

Cut two to three centimetres off each stem on a slant, using sharp scissors, secateurs or a knife. A sharp blade
matters more than the angle, because a blunt one crushes the tubes that draw water up. The slant gives the stem a
little more surface to drink from and stops it sitting flat on the bottom of the vase.

Do it straight away, before the stems have had time to dry out and seal. Woody stems such as roses and
hydrangeas drink better with a fresh cut every couple of days.

## Why take off the leaves below the water?

Leaves sitting in water rot within a day or two, and the bacteria they feed block the stems. Strip anything that
would sit below the waterline. Leave the leaves above it, because they look better and the flower still needs some.

## Does flower food really make a difference?

Yes, and it's the single easiest thing you can do. A sachet of flower food contains sugar to feed the bloom, an
acidifier that helps the stem take up water, and a mild biocide that keeps the water clear. Dissolve the whole
sachet in the amount of water it says on the packet. Too little does very little.

If you've run out, a clean vase and fresh water every day is the next best thing.

## How often should I change the water?

Change it every two days, and top it up in between. Rinse the vase, refill it with cool tap water, re-cut the
stems and put the flowers back. Cloudy or smelly water means bacteria, so change it the moment you see it.

Start with a properly clean vase too. A wash in hot soapy water, or a few drops of household bleach for stubborn
marks, then a good rinse.

## Where's the best place to put a vase of flowers?

Somewhere cool and out of direct sun. Radiators, sunny windowsills, the top of the telly and the oven all shorten
vase life. So do draughts from an open door.

Keep flowers away from the fruit bowl as well. Ripening fruit gives off ethylene, a gas that makes many flowers
age faster. Carnations, alstroemeria, sweet peas and lilies are especially sensitive. Moving the vase to a cooler
room overnight adds days.

## How long do cut flowers last?

It depends on the flower and how fresh it was when it reached you. These are honest ranges for a bouquet looked
after as above.

| Flower | Typical vase life | Good to know |
|---|---|---|
| Carnations | 10 to 21 days | Among the toughest cut flowers |
| Chrysanthemums | 10 to 21 days | Change the water often, the leaves rot quickly |
| Alstroemeria | 10 to 14 days | Pull off spent heads and new buds open |
| Lilies | 7 to 14 days | Pinch out the orange pollen as they open |
| Roses | 5 to 10 days | Garden roses often go faster than long-stem roses |
| Sunflowers | 6 to 12 days | Heavy heads, so use a sturdy vase |
| Tulips | 5 to 10 days | Keep growing in the vase |
| Hydrangeas | 4 to 8 days | Wilt fast if they dry out, but often revive |
| Sweet peas | 3 to 6 days | Short-lived but worth it for the scent |

A mixed bouquet won't all go at once. Pull out stems as they fade and the rest will look fresher for longer.

## Can wilted flowers be revived?

Often, yes. Re-cut the stems and stand them in deep, cool water for an hour or two. Hydrangeas can go in a sink
of cool water, head and all, for half an hour. Roses with drooping heads usually recover the same way, and the
[rose care guide](/blogs/guides/rose-care) has the details, and tulips have [their own quirks](/blogs/guides/tulip-care).

If a flower has gone papery, brown at the edges or mushy at the stem, it's done. Take it out so it doesn't hurry
the others.

## Which flowers are a problem with pets?

Lilies are the big one. Every part of a lily, including the pollen and the vase water, is dangerous to cats. Our
[guide to flowers and pets](/blogs/guides/flowers-and-pets) lists the others to watch.
